Criminology is the systematic study of crime, people who commit crime, victims, and society’s responses to offending.

What criminology studies

Criminology looks at how crime is defined, why it happens, how it affects individuals and communities, and how societies try to prevent and respond to it.

Criminology and criminal justice

Criminology is a field of study. The criminal justice system is the set of institutions and processes—such as policing, courts and corrections—that respond to crime.

Why it matters

Understanding crime can help communities develop fairer laws, improve prevention, support victims and reduce reoffending.

Criminological theories offer different explanations for why crime occurs. No single theory explains every offence or every person.

Classical perspectives

Classical approaches emphasise choice, responsibility and the idea that people may weigh possible benefits against consequences.

Biological and psychological perspectives

These approaches examine how individual development, health, cognition and behaviour may relate to offending. They should never be used to assume that a person is destined to commit crime.

Sociological perspectives

Social learning, strain, inequality, peer groups and community conditions can influence opportunities, pressures and norms connected with offending.

Victimology studies victims, patterns of victimisation, the effects of crime and the services and rights available to people harmed by crime.

Who is a victim?

A victim may be an individual, group or organisation that experiences harm from an offence. The impact can be physical, emotional, financial or social.

Impact and support

People may need medical care, counselling, practical assistance, safety planning or help navigating legal processes.

Respectful practice

Avoid blaming victims. Listen without judgement, protect privacy and recognise that people respond to trauma in different ways.

Why is victim-sensitive communication important during an investigation?

The criminal justice system includes agencies and procedures that investigate alleged offences, determine cases and administer lawful outcomes.

Police and investigation

Police may receive reports, preserve scenes, interview witnesses and gather evidence, subject to applicable law and safeguards.

Courts and due process

Courts assess evidence and apply the law. Fair-trial principles include the presumption of innocence and the right to be heard.

Corrections and rehabilitation

Correctional services administer sentences and may provide education, treatment and reintegration support.

Cybercrime involves unlawful activity in which computers, networks or digital data are the target, tool or setting of an offence.

Cyber-dependent offences

Some offences rely on technology, such as unauthorised access, malware deployment or disruption of computer systems.

Cyber-enabled offences

Other offences, such as fraud, harassment or identity theft, may be amplified or facilitated by digital platforms.

Staying safer online

Use unique passwords, multi-factor authentication, software updates and caution with unexpected links or requests for money.

Cybersecurity is the practice of protecting devices, accounts, networks and information from unauthorised access, damage or disruption.

Confidentiality, integrity and availability

Confidentiality means information is seen only by authorised people. Integrity means it remains accurate and unaltered. Availability means authorised users can access it when needed.

Common threats

Threats include phishing, weak passwords, malicious software, social engineering and unpatched vulnerabilities.

Basic protection

Keep systems updated, back up important files, use reputable security tools and verify unusual requests through a separate trusted channel.

Digital forensics is the careful identification, preservation, examination and reporting of information from digital devices and systems.

Types of digital evidence

Evidence may include messages, emails, files, photographs, metadata, access logs and records from online services.

Preservation

Investigators should avoid unnecessary changes to devices or data and follow lawful procedures for collection and storage.

Integrity and documentation

A documented chain of custody and appropriate integrity checks help show that evidence has not been improperly altered.

Think about it

Why is it important to document who handled digital evidence and when?

Online fraud uses digital communication or services to deceive people or organisations for money, information or another benefit.

Common warning signs

Be cautious of urgency, guaranteed returns, impersonation, unusual payment methods and requests for passwords or one-time codes.

Verification

Check claims using an independently found official contact channel. Do not rely only on links or phone numbers in a suspicious message.

If targeted

Stop communication, preserve relevant messages and transaction details, contact your bank or payment provider promptly and report through appropriate official channels.

Cyberstalking and online harassment involve repeated or threatening behaviour through digital channels that may cause fear, distress or harm.

Examples

Conduct can include repeated unwanted contact, threats, monitoring, impersonation, doxxing or publishing private material without consent.

Safety steps

Review privacy settings, secure accounts, save evidence safely, block or report accounts where appropriate and tell a trusted person.

Support and reporting

Threats or immediate danger should be taken seriously. Seek local support and report through appropriate authorities or platform channels.

Privacy and data protection concern how personal information is collected, used, stored, shared and secured.

Personal information

Personal data can identify someone directly or indirectly. Sensitive information deserves especially careful handling.

Responsible data use

Collect only what is necessary, explain the purpose, restrict access and keep information only as long as justified and lawful.

Ethical practice

Researchers and practitioners should respect dignity, confidentiality, fairness and applicable laws.

Crime prevention aims to reduce opportunities for crime, lower risks of harm and strengthen community safety.

Situational prevention

This approach changes environments or processes to make offending more difficult, risky or less rewarding.

Community partnerships

Trust, communication and cooperation between residents, services and law enforcement can help identify local concerns.

Evidence-led approaches

Prevention should be evaluated for effectiveness, fairness and unintended consequences.

Criminal justice responses may include punishment, rehabilitation, public protection and processes that repair harm.

Purposes of sentencing

Sentencing purposes can include accountability, deterrence, public protection and rehabilitation, depending on the law and case.

Rehabilitation

Education, skills training, treatment and practical reintegration support may help reduce future offending.

Restorative justice

Where lawful, voluntary and safe, restorative processes may give affected people a structured way to discuss harm, responsibility and possible repair.

Think about it

How can rehabilitation benefit both an individual and the wider community?

Criminological research uses systematic methods to understand crime, victimisation, justice processes and prevention.

Quantitative methods

Surveys, official statistics and structured datasets can help identify patterns and compare groups or time periods.

Qualitative methods

Interviews, observations and document analysis can provide deeper insight into experiences and meanings.

Ethics and limitations

Researchers should protect participants, explain consent, consider bias and avoid treating correlation as proof of causation.

Criminology and cybersecurity offer different but sometimes overlapping career paths in public service, research, technology and community support.

Criminology-related roles

Possible paths include research, policy, victim support, probation-related services, crime analysis and justice administration, subject to local qualifications.

Cybersecurity-related roles

Possible paths include security operations, digital forensics, incident response, governance, risk and compliance, and security awareness.

Building a pathway

Study foundational subjects, practise ethical skills in authorised environments, build a portfolio and check the qualifications required in your country.
